      SPHERExLens · explainer

      What is this page, and how do I use it?

      This page looks for things in space that change. Not things that are pretty -- things that move or flicker between two photographs of the same patch of sky.

      The camera

      NASA's SPHEREx telescope photographs the entire sky, then does it again a few months later, in 102 different shades of infrared. Infrared is light our eyes cannot see, but warm and dusty objects glow in it -- so it is very good at finding cold stars and newborn stars hidden behind dust.

      The idea: subtraction

      Take picture A, take picture B of the same spot, and subtract one from the other. Everything that did not change cancels out and leaves grey. Everything that did change is left standing. This is the oldest trick in astronomy -- find the thing that blinked.

      The five steps this page runs

      1. Fetch. Download the telescope images for one patch of sky.
      2. Average. Stack the clean ones together to beat down the noise (several noisy pictures make one crisp one).
      3. Subtract. Compare two visits statistically. A real change stands out; random noise averages away.
      4. Check the catalogues. Ask: is this spot already a known star, known variable, known asteroid? If yes it is KNOWN. If nothing known is there, it is a CANDIDATE -- worth a human look, not yet a discovery.
      5. Test yourself. Plant fake stars of known brightness into the real image and see whether the pipeline finds them. That tells you how faint a change this page could actually detect.
      Why the honesty matters. A bright spot in a difference image is not a discovery. It becomes one only after you have checked the catalogues and shown that your detector would have noticed a real object if one were there. That is exactly what the KNOWN / CANDIDATE tags and the recovery grid are for.

      What the colours on the map mean

      KNOWN -- the catalogues already have something at that position. CANDIDATE -- nothing known there; this is the interesting case. the target you selected.

      Nothing here can break. There is no login, no order of operations to get wrong, and every panel updates itself.

      1. Pick a place in the sky. Use the dropdown at the top of the left column. Barnard's Star is a good one to start with -- it moves faster than any other star in the sky, so the change is real and easy to see.
      2. Look at the picture. The left panel shows that patch of sky. Grey is normal sky; a glowing dot is a change between two visits. Use the mouse wheel to zoom, drag to pan, and hover any coloured dot to see what it is.
      3. Click a dot. The right-hand panel explains it: how sure we are, how far it moved, and whether something was already known there.
      4. Read the table. Every object in the field is listed with a KNOWN or CANDIDATE tag. Type in the search box to find one, or use the filter buttons to see only the unknowns.
      5. Switch the view. SNR / EPOCH-1 / EPOCH-2 / DIFFERENCE shows the raw sky, each visit, and the subtraction itself. The all-sky button puts every field on one map.
      6. Press Recovery Grid. How faint a change could this page have found? It plants fake signals and reports which ones it recovered -- the honest measure of the instrument.
      7. Turn on Auto Demo. It walks through the fields by itself, which is the fastest way to see what the page does.

      Things worth knowing

      • Live from IRSA and Open any field need a small Python server. This deployment is a static copy of the page, so those two are switched off here -- the labels say so. Everything else runs entirely in your browser from pre-computed files.
      • The fields come in two groups: the five brief targets (single nearby objects -- the ones with the real science), and a set of famous objects -- galaxies, nebulae, a quasar -- included to show that the same steps work on anything.
      • Each field is one small patch of sky: about 9 arcminutes across, roughly a third of the width of the full Moon.
      • Nothing you click is sent anywhere. There is no tracking and no account.
      Field
      One patch of sky, about 9 arcminutes across.
      Epoch
      One visit by the telescope. Two epochs = two visits to compare.
      Exposure
      A single photograph. Several are stacked to make one clean image.
      Band
      One colour of infrared light. SPHEREx has 102; this page uses a handful.
      σ (sigma)
      How sure we are. 5σ means "noise would do this about once in 3.5 million tries".
      KNOWN
      The catalogues already list something at that spot.
      CANDIDATE
      Nothing known there. Worth a look -- not yet a discovery.
      Completeness
      What fraction of planted fake objects the pipeline found. Higher is better.
      Recovery grid
      The test that measures completeness across brightness and motion.
      RA / Dec
      The sky's version of longitude and latitude, in degrees.
